A month's supplier invoices sit in one folder on your Mac: a dozen PDFs, no two laid out the same way. One came out of Tally, one off a supplier's billing portal, one is a scan of a printed copy a supplier's clerk emailed you. Turning that folder into a GST invoice PDF to Excel job you can actually reconcile means handling each layout, without retyping a single row.

Retyping the dozen of them is an evening you don't have, and it invites the one transposed digit that turns up later, at return time. Copying text out of Preview or Acrobat and pasting it into Excel doesn't help either. A PDF has no rows and columns inside it, only short runs of text sitting at coordinates on the page, so the paste comes out as one long, unsorted line. Uploading the folder to a converter site solves the layout problem and creates a new one: it hands your supplier names, GSTINs and invoice values to a server you didn't choose. I wouldn't send a month of GST invoices there, and I don't think you should either.

PDFTrail is my app. It reads the text and its position straight out of each file, works out which fragments sit on one line and where the columns break, and does the batch on your Mac. Nothing leaves the machine.

## Why every supplier's invoice looks different

Two suppliers can register the same fields with the GST portal and still produce invoices that look nothing alike, because the layout comes from whatever billing software each one runs. One supplier's invoice puts the taxable value and tax amount in two tight columns on the right. Another spreads the same numbers across a wider table with a separate line for each tax head. A third prints a narrow slip with the numbers stacked instead of side by side. The fields are similar; the geometry is not, and a tool that reads by fixed column position will get some of these right and quietly mangle the rest.

## Born-digital invoices versus a scanned copy

Most of the invoices in that folder are born-digital: generated by billing software and saved or emailed as a PDF, which means the text is stored as positioned characters, not a picture. PDFTrail reads that text directly. There is no OCR step and nothing to guess, because the letters and their coordinates are already sitting in the file.

The scanned one is a different document. A photo of a printed invoice, or a page put through an office scanner, stores pixels, not text, and pixels carry no letters. PDFTrail runs OCR on that page, on your Mac, and it shows you the table it detected in a review step before anything is exported. OCR is good on a clean invoice. It is worth a look before you trust the row.

## What a GST invoice has to show

Layouts differ, but the substance behind them does not: a supplier name and address, an invoice date, the line items being billed, a taxable value, and the tax amounts charged against it. The GST portal's own returns section lists an [Invoice Management System](https://www.gst.gov.in/help/returns) among its return processes. That's a reminder that this same invoice, once it leaves your folder, is also expected on the other side of the transaction. I can't tell you the exact column order your suppliers use, because it varies by supplier and by billing software, and I don't have their invoices in front of me.

## GST invoice PDF to Excel, one folder at a time

1. Drop the whole invoice folder onto PDFTrail, not one file at a time.
2. Wait for the read. Born-digital invoices are read directly. A scanned one runs OCR first, on the Mac.
3. Open the review screen for each invoice and check that the supplier name, date, and amounts landed in the right columns.
4. Export the batch to .xlsx, one sheet per invoice.

## What to check in the combined sheet

Once the workbook is open, walk each sheet the same way. Add the line items together and compare the sum against the taxable value printed on the invoice. Then add the tax amounts and compare that total against the tax shown on the page. Where those two additions don't land on the printed figures, you've found a row that needs a second look, and you've found it before it goes anywhere near a return.

This is a check you do in the spreadsheet, invoice by invoice, not something the export decides for you. It takes longer on the invoice with nine line items than on the one with two, and it's worth doing on both.

Excel for Mac won't shortcut this for you either. The "From PDF" import in Power Query, the one that reads a PDF table directly, was built for Windows and was never brought to the Mac version, [as Microsoft's own forum confirms](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/5116197). If you've gone looking for that button on a Mac, that's why it isn't there.
